### Thread context
User is actively redesigning a narrow side patio / courtyard corridor beside a storage shed and orange wooden privacy fence. Multiple photos were reviewed from progressively wider angles. The design direction emerged from the actual geometry of the space rather than a generic garden plan.

### Existing spatial structure
- Left side: beige exterior wall / storage shed, currently exposed utility and tool clutter.
- Center: narrow pale concrete walking lane. This should remain visually open and uninterrupted.
- Right side: orange wooden privacy fence with a thin brick-edged planting strip filled with river rock / stones.
- Back corner: tall curved metal corner plant shelf that has become the visual focal point / destination at the end of the corridor.
- Existing foliage includes climbing / trailing vines, spider-plant-like foliage, leafy potted plants, a fern-like plant, succulents / cactus-like plants, and assorted small pots.
- Upper zone opens visually to tree canopy and sky beyond the fence, which gives the narrow corridor depth.

### Design logic established in thread
1. Treat the corridor as three bands: left utility wall, center clear path, right planted edge.
2. Preserve the concrete as negative space. Do not crowd it with plants or loose pots.
3. Keep the right planting bed narrow and dense rather than widening it.
4. Use the existing brick edge as the hard visual boundary between path and garden.
5. Consolidate loose river stones into a tighter continuous ribbon so the bed reads intentional rather than scattered.
6. Build upward rather than outward. Vertical growth is the main expansion strategy because floor width is limited.
7. Train existing vines horizontally along the fence top and vertically down / around the shelf to create a living wall / canopy effect.
8. The back shelf is now the focal chamber / visual altar at the end of the passage. It supplies height, layering, and a destination for the eye.
9. Consolidate tiny or empty pots. Empty / half-filled containers currently create visual noise. Either plant them, group them intentionally, or remove them until needed.
10. Move floor plants, including the fern-like plant and loose pots, out of the center walkway and into the rock bed or onto lower shelves.
11. The long vine on the right can remain somewhat sparse because it creates a useful vertical line that balances the heavier foliage on the left.
12. Keep the orange fence color. It works strongly against the green foliage, terracotta pots, stone, and pale concrete.
13. Delay obsessive cleanup of the concrete until major planting / construction moves are done, then perform one strong final cleanup pass.

### Visual composition discovered
The corridor naturally creates a compression-to-reveal effect: a tight walkway draws the eye toward the back shelf, then upward into vines, tree canopy, and open sky. The goal should not be to disguise the narrowness, but to exploit it as a hidden passage / secret courtyard experience.

### Left-side utility issue
The garden composition is structurally much stronger than the exposed storage / tool zone on the left. Recommended solution is visual containment rather than necessarily moving the entire utility area. Possible future treatments include a narrow reed screen, lattice, planted screen, or other slim masking layer attached to / beside the shed, provided it does not consume the walking lane.
